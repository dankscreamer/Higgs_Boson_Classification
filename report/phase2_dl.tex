\begin{abstract}
Phase~1 established calibrated, statistically tested machine-learning baselines on the UCI HIGGS
benchmark. Phase~2 asks whether a compact multilayer perceptron (MLP) can learn the interactions
that physicists encode as invariant masses, what its design choices contribute, and whether a
hybrid of the two paradigms is better than either. We train an MLP with batch normalisation,
dropout and Glorot initialisation on stratified subsets of 50k to 10.5M events, ablate 12 design
choices over three seeds, and propose two hybrids motivated by measured Phase~1 behaviour:
(1)~a physics-informed \emph{wide~\&~deep} network in which the expert masses bypass the deep tower,
and (2)~a \emph{stacked} XGBoost+MLP ensemble with a logistic meta-learner fit on a held-out
calibration split. All models share one 500k-event test set. Comparisons use paired DeLong tests
over five seeds; results are in Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:discussion}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Signal is a heavy Higgs boson decaying to $W^+W^-b\bar b$; background is $t\bar t$ production with
the same visible final state \cite{baldi2014searching}. Physicists summarise each event with 7
invariant masses. Baldi \emph{et al.} reported that deep networks on the 21 raw measurements
recover, and even exceed, the information in those masses \cite{baldi2014searching}. In Phase~1 we
found that tuned gradient-boosted trees gain much more from expert or symmetry-derived features than
from raw measurements, and that their probabilities are well calibrated out of the box.
Phase~2 asks:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ4.} At equal data budgets, how does a compact MLP compare with the tuned tree ensemble on each representation, and how does the gap scale with data?
\item \textbf{RQ5.} Which architectural and regularisation choices matter (ablations), and does the Glorot initialisation argument still hold once batch normalisation is used?
\item \textbf{RQ6.} Is there a \emph{technically justified} hybrid that beats both paradigms?
\end{itemize}

\section{Deep-Learning Literature Review}\label{sec:lit}
\subsection{Foundations}
Feed-forward networks with one hidden layer are universal approximators \cite{hornik1989multilayer},
but depth makes compositional functions, such as invariant masses built from sums of four-vectors,
cheaper to represent. Glorot and Bengio showed that saturating activations and naive initialisation
make the variance of activations and gradients shrink or explode with depth. They proposed
$\mathrm{Var}[W]=2/(n_\mathrm{in}+n_\mathrm{out})$ to keep both stable \cite{glorot2010understanding}.
He \emph{et al.} adapted the argument to ReLUs \cite{he2015delving}. Batch normalisation
\cite{ioffe2015batch} re-standardises each layer's pre-activations. Santurkar \emph{et al.} argue that
its benefit comes from a smoother loss landscape rather than reduced covariate shift
\cite{santurkar2018how}. Dropout \cite{srivastava2014dropout} trains an implicit ensemble of thinned
networks. Adam \cite{kingma2015adam} adapts per-parameter step sizes. With Adam, L2 weight decay is
coupled to the adaptive scaling \cite{loshchilov2019decoupled}. Early stopping is an effective
regulariser \cite{prechelt1998early}. Modern networks are often over-confident, and temperature or
Platt scaling fixes most of this \cite{guo2017calibration}.

\subsection{Deep learning on tabular data and in HEP}
On generic tabular benchmarks, tuned tree ensembles remain strong
\cite{grinsztajn2022tree,shwartzziv2022tabular}. Well-regularised MLPs and ResNet-style networks are
competitive baselines \cite{gorishniy2021revisiting}, and specialised architectures exist
\cite{arik2021tabnet,popov2020neural}. In particle physics, deep networks have succeeded where the
inputs are smooth, high-dimensional physical measurements: the HIGGS and $\tau\tau$ benchmarks
\cite{baldi2014searching,baldi2015tautau}, jet tagging \cite{kasieczka2019toptagging}, and
symmetry-respecting architectures such as Energy Flow Networks \cite{komiske2019energyflow}. Reviews
in \cite{guest2018deep,radovic2018machine} survey the field.

\subsection{Hybrid models}
Stacked generalisation \cite{wolpert1992stacked} trains a meta-learner on held-out base predictions.
Facebook's ads system feeds boosted-tree leaves into a linear model \cite{he2014practical}. Wide \&
deep learning \cite{cheng2016wide} combines a ``wide'' path for memorising known, engineered
cross-features with a ``deep'' path for generalising. Our first hybrid adapts this idea to physics:
the expert masses are the engineered knowledge.

\section{Architecture Logic}\label{sec:arch}
\subsection{Baseline MLP}
Inputs are standardised on training rows only. The network is
$[\text{Linear}\to\text{BatchNorm}\to\text{ReLU}\to\text{Dropout}(0.1)]\times3$ with 256 units,
followed by a linear logit (139k parameters on 28 inputs). We chose these settings for the following
reasons.
(i)~\emph{Depth 3, width 256}: enough capacity to compose pairwise quantities
(e.g.\ $p_{T,a}p_{T,b}\cos\Delta\phi_{ab}$) into mass-like features, yet small enough to train in
seconds at 250k events. Both settings are ablated.
(ii)~\emph{BatchNorm}: keeps every layer's input standardised, which permits a larger learning rate
and reduces sensitivity to initialisation.
(iii)~\emph{Dropout and weight decay}: mild, on the reasoning that 200k training events against 139k
parameters is not a heavily over-parameterised regime. The ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablations})
shows this reasoning was too optimistic: dropout 0.3 is better.
(iv)~\emph{Glorot-uniform initialisation}: the pre-read's prescription, ablated against PyTorch's
default Kaiming-uniform.
(v)~\emph{Training}: binary cross-entropy, Adam ($10^{-3}$), batch 1024, learning rate halved when
validation AUC plateaus for 2 epochs, early stopping after 6 epochs without improvement, and the best
weights restored.

In a plain MLP on \texttt{geo+high} the 7 masses are just 7 of 47 first-layer inputs, and the network
must learn to route them through three non-linear layers. In the wide \& deep model
(Fig.~\ref{fig:widedeep}) the masses take a short path to the output. The deep tower is then free to
learn \emph{residual} geometry that the masses do not capture. The control is the plain MLP on
exactly the same 47 inputs, so any difference is due to the architecture alone.

\subsection{Hybrid 2: stacked XGBoost + MLP}
The rationale comes from the measured behaviour of the two base models at the fixed 250k budget
(Table~\ref{tab:dl_main}). They (a)~reach similar AUCs (0.838 vs.\ 0.842 on \texttt{geo+high})
through different mechanisms, axis-aligned splits versus smooth interactions, and so are expected to
make partly different errors; and (b)~differ in calibration (ECE 0.005 vs.\ 0.036). A logistic meta-learner on their logits,
\begin{equation}
p_\mathrm{stack}=\sigma\bigl(\beta_0+\beta_1\,\mathrm{logit}\,p_\mathrm{XGB}+\beta_2\,\mathrm{logit}\,p_\mathrm{MLP}\bigr),
\end{equation}
is fit on the cal split, which neither base model saw: XGBoost and the MLP early-stop on val. This
gives three properties. It is \emph{calibrated by construction}, because it is fit by log loss on
held-out data. Its weights $\beta_{1,2}$ are \emph{interpretable} as each model's trustworthiness.
And it cannot overfit the test set. The naive control is the unweighted average of the two
probabilities.

\section{DL Dataset Pipeline and Regularisation}\label{sec:data}
The 11M-event file is converted once to a memory-mapped float32 array, so any stratified subset is
an index lookup, and moving from 50k events to the full 10.5M pool needs no new loading code. Each
seed draws a stratified subset and splits it 80/10/10 into train, val (early stopping) and cal
(calibration and stacking). The last 500k events are the fixed test set \cite{baldi2014searching}.
Regularisation is layered: batch normalisation, dropout, L2 weight decay, early stopping on validation
AUC with the best weights restored, and learning-rate decay on plateau. The ablations
(Section~\ref{sec:ablations}) remove each one in turn. The data budget is itself a regulariser, and
it is varied in Section~\ref{sec:scaling}.

\textbf{Platform note.} Networks are implemented in PyTorch \cite{paszke2019pytorch}. On macOS, the
xgboost wheel and PyTorch each bundle an OpenMP runtime, and
multi-threaded PyTorch CPU kernels deadlock when both are loaded. The MLP therefore trains on the
Apple-silicon GPU (MPS) with one CPU thread. This affects only wall-clock comparisons and is
documented in the repository.

\section{Theoretical Notes}\label{sec:theory}
\textbf{Glorot initialisation.} For a linear layer $\mathbf z=W\mathbf x$ with independent zero-mean
weights, $\mathrm{Var}[z]=n_\mathrm{in}\mathrm{Var}[W]\mathrm{Var}[x]$ in the forward pass and
$\mathrm{Var}[\partial\mathcal L/\partial x]=n_\mathrm{out}\mathrm{Var}[W]\mathrm{Var}[\partial\mathcal L/\partial z]$
in the backward pass. Keeping both ratios at~1 is impossible unless $n_\mathrm{in}=n_\mathrm{out}$, and
the compromise is $\mathrm{Var}[W]=2/(n_\mathrm{in}+n_\mathrm{out})$, i.e.\
$W\sim U[-\sqrt{6/(n_\mathrm{in}+n_\mathrm{out})},+\sqrt{6/(n_\mathrm{in}+n_\mathrm{out})}]$
\cite{glorot2010understanding}. The argument assumes approximately linear (tanh-like) units and no
normalisation. With BatchNorm, every pre-activation is re-standardised and the initial weight scale
largely cancels. We therefore expect initialisation to matter for the \emph{tanh, no-BatchNorm}
network and hardly at all for the baseline. The ablation tests exactly this.

\textbf{Batch normalisation.}
$\hat z=(z-\mu_\mathcal B)/\sqrt{\sigma^2_\mathcal B+\epsilon}$, $y=\gamma\hat z+\beta$, using mini-batch
statistics during training and running averages at test time. The noise in mini-batch statistics
also acts as a mild regulariser.

\textbf{Dropout.} Each unit is zeroed with probability $p$ during training and outputs are rescaled
by $1/(1-p)$. This approximates averaging over $2^{H}$ thinned networks \cite{srivastava2014dropout},
reducing co-adaptation.

\textbf{Why stacking in logit space.} If each base model's logit is approximately an affine function
of the true log-likelihood ratio plus partly independent noise, the Bayes-optimal combination is a
weighted sum of logits. A logistic meta-learner estimates exactly those weights, while averaging
probabilities fixes them at $\tfrac12$ and operates in the wrong space.

\textbf{Uncertainty.} AUC confidence intervals and paired tests use DeLong's covariance on the shared
test set \cite{delong1988comparing,sun2014fast}. Each comparison is repeated over seeds.
